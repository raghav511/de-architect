## O5.1 What the blueprint is

The capstone is one document, the **data foundation blueprint**, for a company you know or for this module's design problem. It is what a staff or principal engineer writes when asked "what should our data platform be for the next three years, and how do we get there?". It has ten parts:

| Part | What it answers | Draws on |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Business and numbers | What the data is for, who uses it, how much there is | 10.3, 11 |
| 2. Principles | The few rules every later decision follows | R1, O1 |
| 3. Reference architecture | Each layer and the technology chosen for it | 1–8, 10 |
| 4. Data products and owners | Which domains own which products | O1, M1–M3 |
| 5. Platform and golden paths | What teams get to build on | O2 |
| 6. Governance | Classification, access, lineage, retention, audit | O3 |
| 7. Service levels and operations | The platform's SLOs, on-call, incidents | O4, 9 |
| 8. Capacity and cost | Peak numbers, sizing, cost per year and per unit | 10.3, 7, O4 |
| 9. Roadmap | The first 90 days, the year, the migration | R1, 6.8 |
| 10. Risks and decisions | What could go wrong; each decision with its alternative | All |

You also run it: the simulator's **foundation** challenge is the blueprint as a working system under launch-day load, and it must pass.

## O5.2 Start from the business and its numbers

Every good blueprint starts with what the company is trying to do, not with tools. Write down:

- **The decisions data must support:** pricing, fraud, supply and demand, finance close, product experiments, AI features.
- **The consumers:** how many analysts, dashboards, ML models, product features, external partners.
- **The sources:** operational databases, event streams, files from partners, SaaS tools.
- **The numbers:** events a day and at peak, bytes per event, tables, growth per year, freshness each consumer needs, how long data must be kept, regions.
- **The constraints:** regulation (GDPR, PCI, local data residency), budget, team size, existing contracts.

If you cannot write the numbers, estimate them out loud with the arithmetic from 10.3; an interviewer or a CTO checks that the design follows from them.

## O5.3 Principles

Principles are the few rules that settle later arguments. Good ones are specific enough to say no to something:

1. **Open table formats as the system of record**: data lives in Iceberg or Delta on object storage; engines come and go.
2. **Domains own their data products**, with contracts and SLOs; the platform owns the paved roads (O1, O2).
3. **Log first**: every change in an operational system reaches the platform through CDC or events, never through nightly dumps or dual writes (5, 6).
4. **Freshness to match the decision**: streaming where seconds change a decision, batch everywhere else (5.10).
5. **Governance as code**: tags, policies and retention enforced by the platform, not by review boards (O3).
6. **Cost is a feature**: every resource tagged, every team sees its spend, unit costs reported monthly (O4).
7. **Buy the commodity, build the differentiator**: managed services for undifferentiated plumbing, your own code where the business wins.

Five to eight principles is enough. Each should be one that someone could reasonably disagree with; otherwise it says nothing.

## O5.4 The reference architecture, layer by layer

Draw the layers and choose one primary technology for each, with the alternative you rejected and why:

| Layer | Job | Typical choice | Module |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sources | Apps, databases, events, files, SaaS | — | 1 |
| Ingestion | CDC, event collection, file landing, SaaS connectors | Debezium and Kafka; managed connectors for SaaS | 5, 6 |
| Storage | The system of record for analytics | Iceberg or Delta on object storage with a REST catalog | 2, 8 |
| Processing | Batch and streaming transformations | Spark or the warehouse with dbt for batch; Flink or Spark streaming | 3, 4, 5 |
| Serving | Dashboards, APIs, ML features, AI | Warehouse or Trino; OLAP for live dashboards; key-value for product reads; vector store for RAG | 8, 10, A5 |
| Orchestration | Scheduling and dependencies | Airflow | 1 |
| Governance | Catalog, lineage, policies, quality | A catalog with OpenLineage; tag-based policies in every engine; contracts in CI | O3, 4 |
| Observability | Freshness, volume, cost, incidents | SLO monitors, cost reports, a status page | 9, O4 |

Then draw the flow end to end: apps write to Postgres, CDC carries changes into Kafka beside product events, a stream job keeps serving stores fresh and lands history in the lakehouse, batch jobs build the curated models, and analysts, products and models read through governed interfaces. That flow is what the simulator's foundation challenge runs.

## O5.5 Data products and owners

Map the business domains to the data products they will own (O1), and say which products stay central:

| Domain | Source-aligned products | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Orders | orders, payments, refunds | Orders team |
| Trips | trips, driver locations | Trips team |
| Marketing | campaigns, attribution | Marketing data team |
| Company-wide | revenue, executive scorecard, customers (reference) | Central analytics |

For each important product give its grain, consumers and one SLO. Name the shared identifiers (customer, city, currency) that make products joinable, and which domain issues each.

## O5.6 The platform and governance

Describe what teams get, in the words of O2 to O4:

- **Golden paths** for the top journeys: new batch data product, new CDC source, new streaming job, access request; each a template with contract, tests, CI, deployment, monitors and catalog registration.
- **Governance as code:** tags declared in contracts and detected by a scanner, tag-based masking and row rules in every engine, OpenLineage into the catalog, retention by class and erasure with snapshot expiry, access through a workflow with expiry, audit logs.
- **Service levels for the platform:** ingestion freshness, job success, query latency, availability; an error budget policy; burn-rate alerts; a status page.
- **Showback:** tags on every resource, unit costs, a monthly report per team.

## O5.7 Capacity and cost

Size every layer for the **peak**, not the average, with the arithmetic of 10.3:

- Events per second at peak, bytes per event, MB/s into Kafka; partitions at a few MB/s each with headroom; stream tasks to match.
- Database writes and reads at peak, and the replicas they need.
- Storage per year raw and compressed, by tier; retention by class.
- Queries per hour, bytes scanned per query, warehouse or engine capacity.

Then price it per year: compute, storage, transfer, licences and people. Give unit costs (per TB scanned, per data product per month, per event) and compare at least two options for the expensive layers, such as a managed warehouse against Iceberg on Trino, over three years including the people to run each. The cheapest option in year one is often not the cheapest over three.

## O5.8 The roadmap: 90 days, a year, and the migration

A blueprint without a plan is a wish. Write:

- **First 90 days:** the foundations everything else needs (catalog, contracts on the top sources, CDC for the two busiest databases, the first golden path, cost tags), and one visible win for a real consumer.
- **The year:** quarter by quarter, in dependency order, within the team's capacity; each item with the outcome it should move.
- **The migration** from what exists today (R1, 6.8): inventory, dual run with reconciliation, consumers moved in groups, old systems retired with dates.
- **What you are not doing**, and why.

## O5.9 Architect's lens: the data foundation blueprint

The questions an architect is asked about a data foundation blueprint: what it is for, how to make it real, and how it fails.

| Question | Short answer for the foundation blueprint |
|---|---|
| **What problem does it solve?** | A company whose data grew team by team: many copies, unclear owners, surprise costs, slow new datasets and no single answer to "what is our revenue?". The blueprint sets one direction (principles, architecture, ownership, platform, governance, cost and a plan) that every team can follow. |
| **Why it over the alternatives?** | Against no plan: teams keep solving the same problems separately. Against a big-bang rebuild: a staged roadmap with dual runs delivers value every quarter and can stop safely. Against copying another company's architecture: the blueprint follows this company's numbers, constraints and skills. |
| **When not to use it?** | A startup with one data engineer and one database needs a page of principles, not a three-year blueprint. Write the full blueprint when several teams, real cost and regulation are involved. |
| **Who owns and pays for it?** | A staff or principal engineer writes it with the platform lead, domain leads, security and finance, and the head of data or CTO approves it. It is funded as a programme with quarterly outcomes, and reviewed every half year. |
| **Where does it sit?** | Above the individual designs: each new pipeline, product or tool decision is checked against its principles and architecture, and its decisions section records why the foundation is shaped as it is. |
| **How does it fail and scale?** | It scales by delegation: principles and golden paths let teams decide locally. It fails as a document nobody reads, a plan without numbers, a tool shopping list, a big bang that never ships, and a design that ignores the people who must run it. |
| **When do you move off it?** | You revise it rather than abandon it: when the business, scale, regulation or technology change enough that a principle or a layer no longer fits, write a new decision, update the blueprint and say what changed. |

### Under the hood

**1. How do you size the event backbone for a foundation?**
<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

From the peak, not the average: events per second at peak (daily total ÷ 86,400 × the peak factor) times bytes per event gives MB/s. Divide by what one partition and one consumer task handle (a few MB/s) and add headroom of about 1.5, rounding up. Brokers must carry that rate times the replication factor. Retention in days times MB/s times replication gives disk, or tiered storage moves older segments to object storage. Then run it: the simulator's [foundation challenge](#/sim/sc/foundation) fails on launch day until partitions, tasks, database replicas and warehouse clusters are sized for four times the normal load.

</details>

**2. How do you choose between a managed warehouse and an open lakehouse for the foundation?**
<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Start from workloads and constraints, then compare total cost over three years. A managed warehouse wins on zero tuning, high-concurrency dashboards and a small team; an open lakehouse wins on one copy read by many engines, lower storage cost, ML and streaming access to the same tables, and less lock-in. Many foundations do both: open tables as the system of record, and a warehouse or OLAP store for hot, high-concurrency marts. Count the people needed to run each, not only the compute, and say what would change the decision.

</details>

**3. What goes in the first 90 days of a foundation programme?**
<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

What everything else depends on, plus one visible win. Foundations: the catalog and its ownership model, cost tags and the first showback report, contracts and CDC on the two or three busiest sources, the first golden path (a new batch data product), and the platform's first SLOs. The win: one consumer, such as finance's daily revenue or a product team's dashboard, served from a certified data product faster and more reliably than before. Say what you are deliberately not doing yet.

</details>

**4. How do you get a blueprint adopted rather than ignored?**
<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Write it with the people who must follow it: domain leads, the platform team, security and finance review drafts. Keep it short, lead with decisions and reasons, and show the alternatives. Make it executable: golden paths and guardrails turn principles into the easiest way to work, so following the blueprint is faster than ignoring it. Tie it to outcomes leadership already wants (time to a new dataset, trusted revenue numbers, cost per query) and report them quarterly. Revisit it every half year with a dated change log.

</details>

**5. How do you migrate to the new foundation without stopping the business?**
<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Strangler, domain by domain: build the new platform beside the old, move one domain's sources through CDC, dual-run the old and new tables and reconcile them daily by key and totals until they match, then move consumers in groups with a rollback path, and retire the old tables and jobs with announced dates. Start with a domain that has engineers and real consumers, measure each step, and keep the old system read-only until the new one has run clean for a full reporting cycle such as a month-end close.

</details>

**6. What risks would you list in a foundation blueprint, and what do you do about each?**
<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

People: too few data engineers in domains (embed engineers, an enabling team, hiring in the plan). Adoption: teams keep old ways (golden paths that are faster, retire support for old ways with dates). Cost: spend grows faster than value (tags, showback, unit costs, budgets per domain). Migration: numbers do not match (dual runs and reconciliation before any cutover). Vendor or technology: a managed service changes price or terms (open formats, a tested exit). Regulation: a new residency or AI rule (governance as code, region-aware storage). Each risk gets an owner and an early warning number.

</details>

## O5.10 Presenting and defending the blueprint

The last step is a review, like a staff design review or the final round of a loop. In 10 minutes:

1. **The problem in numbers** (1 minute): what is slow, costly or untrusted today, and for whom.
2. **The principles** (1 minute): the few rules, and one thing they say no to.
3. **The architecture** (3 minutes): the end-to-end flow, the choice per layer and one rejected alternative each.
4. **Ownership, platform and governance** (2 minutes): who owns what, what teams get, how data is protected.
5. **Cost and the plan** (2 minutes): cost per year and per unit, the first 90 days and the year.
6. **Risks** (1 minute): the top three and what you will watch.

Expect follow-ups: what breaks at ten times the load, what you would cut with half the budget, why not another tool, how a region outage is handled, and what you would do differently next time. Answer with numbers and trade-offs, and say "I don't know, here is how I would find out" when that is true.

TITLE: Read and fix: the capacity plan that rounds down
MIN: 10
SPEC: Return how many Kafka partitions the backbone needs at peak: peak events a second times event size gives MB/s; divide by what one partition handles, multiply by the headroom, and round **up** to a whole partition. Never return fewer than one.
CODE:
```
def partitions_needed(peak_events, event_kb, per_partition_mb, headroom=1.5):
    mb_per_s = peak_events * event_kb / 1000
    return int(mb_per_s / per_partition_mb * headroom)
```
FIX:
```
import math


def partitions_needed(peak_events, event_kb, per_partition_mb, headroom=1.5):
    mb_per_s = peak_events * event_kb / 1000
    return max(1, math.ceil(mb_per_s / per_partition_mb * headroom))
```

My usual answer is both: open tables as the record, managed compute where it saves people, and a warehouse or OLAP store for hot marts. I would change it if concurrency, team size, residency rules or prices changed.

Q: What would you cut with half the budget?
TAG: arch
A: Keep what everything depends on: the catalog, contracts and CDC on the busiest sources, cost tags, and the first golden path. Delay or drop the expensive and the optional: the second streaming use case if minutes are acceptable, the separate OLAP store if the warehouse can serve the dashboards, the self-run components in favour of managed ones. Say what each cut gives up (freshness, a team's speed, a later date) and which outcome moves more slowly.
---
Q: How would you migrate from the current warehouse to this foundation?
TAG: arch
A: Domain by domain with the strangler pattern: platform first, then one domain's sources through CDC into the new tables, dual-run old and new with daily reconciliation by key and totals, move consumers in groups with a rollback path, and retire old tables with dates. Start with a domain that has engineers and real consumers, keep the old system read-only through a full month-end close, and report progress in numbers each month.

Q: What are the biggest risks in your blueprint?
TAG: arch
A: People (too few data engineers in domains: embedding and hiring in the plan), adoption (teams keep old ways: faster golden paths and retirement dates), cost (spend outruns value: tags, showback, unit costs), migration (numbers do not match: dual runs before cutover), vendors (price or terms change: open formats and a tested exit) and regulation (new residency or AI rules: governance as code). Each has an owner and an early warning number.

Q: Tell me about the largest design you have led end to end. What would you do differently?
TAG: behavioral
A: Answer with your own STAR story. A strong shape: the business problem in numbers; how you gathered requirements and wrote the design with the people who would run it; two key decisions with the alternatives you rejected; how you delivered it in stages; the measured result; and an honest, specific change you would make, such as starting the migration reconciliation earlier or involving finance sooner.

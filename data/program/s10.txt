## 10.1 What a design round tests

Design prompts appear in nearly every file in your bank. Uber, Stripe, LinkedIn, Atlassian, Nvidia, Adobe and Google all run a full design round for senior data engineers. The prompts are short on purpose: "a streaming pipeline for real-time insights", "an IoT ingestion pipeline", "a fraud detection system".

The interviewer is not looking for one right architecture. They score four things:

1. **Do you clarify before you build?** A senior engineer finds out what is needed before choosing tools.
2. **Are your choices justified?** Every box comes with a reason and a rejected alternative.
3. **Do you think about failure?** What happens when a component dies, data is late, or volume doubles.
4. **Do you use numbers?** Throughput, storage, latency and cost, estimated aloud.

You have built most of these systems in the last nine sprints. This sprint teaches you to present them.

## 10.2 A method for 45 minutes

| Step | Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Clarify | 5–8 | Functional: what data comes in, what must come out, for whom. Non-functional: volume, freshness, correctness, retention, access, cost limits. Write the answers where the interviewer can see them. |
| 2. Estimate | 3–5 | Events per second at peak, bytes per day, storage per year, partitions or shards needed |
| 3. Draw the flow | 8–10 | Sources, ingest, raw storage, processing, serving, consumers. Orchestration and monitoring around it. One sentence per box. |
| 4. Go deep | 10–15 | Pick, or let the interviewer pick, two components and explain them fully: schema, partitioning, guarantees |
| 5. Break it | 5 | Failure of each component, late and duplicate data, 10× growth |
| 6. Close | 2–3 | Trade-offs you made, and what you would build next |

Two habits help. Say what you are assuming when the interviewer does not answer: "I'll assume 50,000 events per second at peak." And state the guarantee the system gives: "Dashboards are at most 30 seconds behind and may revise for two minutes; the daily numbers are final."

## 10.3 Capacity arithmetic at staff scale

Memorise a few numbers and the rest is multiplication. At staff level the interviewer expects billions of events a day, petabytes, regions and money, so the numbers below go that far.

| Fact | Value |
|---|---|
| Seconds in a day | 86,400, about 10⁵ |
| 1 billion events a day | about 11,600 per second |
| 100 billion events a day | about 1.2 million per second |
| A typical event | 0.5 to 1 KB as JSON |
| Parquet compared with JSON | often 5 to 10 times smaller |
| Peak compared with average | plan for 2 to 3 times |
| One Kafka partition | plan for about 5 to 10 MB/s in, and one consumer task reading it at a few MB/s |
| One Kafka broker | a few hundred MB/s in, before replication; replication factor 3 triples the disk and network writes |
| S3 request rate | about 3,500 writes (PUT, COPY, POST, DELETE) and 5,500 reads (GET, HEAD) per second **per prefix**; more prefixes, more rate |
| S3 object size for analytics | 128 MB to 1 GB per file; millions of small files cost requests and planning time |
| Memory, SSD, same-zone network, cross-region | about 0.1 µs, 100 µs, 0.5 ms and 50 to 150 ms |

### Unit costs to reason with

Rough public list prices in US regions, late 2026. They change and vary by provider and discount, so say "about" and show the arithmetic; the interviewer checks the method, not the cent.

| Item | About |
|---|---|
| Object storage, standard | $23 per TB-month |
| Object storage, infrequent access | $12 per TB-month (plus a retrieval charge) |
| Object storage, archive (instant or deep) | $4 to $1 per TB-month (deep archive takes hours to read) |
| S3 requests | $5 per million writes, $0.40 per million reads |
| Query by data scanned (BigQuery on demand, Athena) | $5 to $6.25 per TB scanned |
| Compute, on-demand | about $0.04 to $0.05 per vCPU-hour |
| Compute, spot or preemptible | about a third of on-demand, can be taken away |
| Data out to the internet | about $0.09 per GB ($90 per TB) |
| Between regions | about $0.02 per GB ($20 per TB) |
| Between zones in a region | about $0.01 per GB each way |

### Worked examples

**A. One million events a second.** A product with 100 billion events a day averages about 1.2 million a second; use 1 million as the planning number and 2 to 3 million at peak. At 1 KB each that is about 1 GB/s, or 86 TB a day raw. Kafka: at about 4 MB/s per partition, 1 GB/s needs 250 partitions, so plan 300 to 500 across brokers that each take a few hundred MB/s (with replication factor 3, about 3 GB/s of disk writes cluster-wide). One stream task per partition. As Parquet, about 10 TB a day, so about 3.6 PB a year, roughly $80,000 a month of standard storage after a year unless older data moves to cheaper tiers. *Simulator:* [One million events a second](#/sim/sc/million) fails at 128 partitions until you size them.

**B. A multi-petabyte backfill.** Recompute three years of a 2.5 PB-a-year events table: 7.5 PB raw, about 1.5 PB as compressed Parquet to read. Scanning it in a pay-per-TB engine at about $5 per TB costs about $7,500 a pass, and a mistake means paying again. On your own Spark cluster: if one vCPU reads about 50 MB/s of Parquet, 1.5 PB is 30 million vCPU-seconds, about 8,300 vCPU-hours, so about $400 on demand or about $140 on spot, plus the S3 reads (1.5 PB in 512 MB files is 3 million GETs, about $1.20). The lesson: compute is cheap, scanning someone else's engine per TB is not, and an idempotent, partition-by-partition backfill can run on spot. Writes: 20,000 objects a second under one prefix gets throttled at 3,500, so spread keys over prefixes. *Simulator:* [Backfill on a budget](#/sim/sc/petabyte) and [S3 request rate per prefix](#/sim/sc/prefix).

**C. Multi-region, active-active.** Two regions both take writes. State the targets first: **RPO** (how much data you may lose, as time) and **RTO** (how long you may be down). Asynchronous replication across regions adds 50 to 150 ms plus whatever the replication backlog is, so RPO is "seconds", not zero; zero RPO needs synchronous writes across regions and costs that latency on every write. Conflicts need a rule: one home region per key (users pinned to a region), or last-writer-wins with clocks you trust, or CRDTs for counters. Cost: every byte replicated pays inter-region transfer, so 10 TB a day across regions is about $200 a day. *Simulator:* [Lose a region: RPO and RTO](#/sim/sc/region).

**D. A warehouse that charges by TB scanned.** Dashboards running 2,000 queries an hour, each scanning 50 GB of an unpartitioned table, scan 100 TB an hour: about $500 an hour, $360,000 a month. Partitioning by date and clustering by the filter column so each query reads 1 GB cuts it fifty-fold; a daily aggregate table that queries read instead cuts it again. Always turn "per TB" into "per month" out loud.

The interviewer will rarely check your arithmetic. They check that you did it, and that your design follows from it: at 3,000 events per second, a single database could take the writes, so if you propose Kafka you should say why; at 1 million a second, you should say how many partitions and why.

## 10.4 Choosing a serving store

Processing produces results. Something must serve them. The choice depends on the access pattern, so name the pattern first.

| Access pattern | Store | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Look up one key in milliseconds, at very high rate | **Redis** | In memory; for counters, sessions, the latest value per entity |
| Look up by key, durable, scaling without operations | **DynamoDB** | Partition key spreads load, sort key orders items within it; design tables for known queries |
| Very high write volume, queried by known keys | **Cassandra** | Partition key decides the node, clustering columns order rows; one table per query pattern |
| Aggregations over fresh events, many concurrent users, sub-second | **Pinot, Druid, ClickHouse** | Columnar, ingest directly from Kafka; Uber uses Pinot for user-facing analytics |
| Text search, log exploration | **Elasticsearch, OpenSearch** | Inverted indexes |
| Complex analytical SQL, seconds acceptable | **The warehouse** | Snowflake, Redshift, BigQuery, or Trino over the lake |

The common mistake is pointing a live product feature at the warehouse. Warehouses handle heavy queries well and thousands of concurrent small queries badly.

## 10.5 Distributed systems vocabulary

Several of your bank's questions test these terms directly.

**CAP.** When a network partition separates nodes, a distributed store must choose between staying **consistent**, by refusing some requests, and staying **available**, by answering with possibly stale data. Cassandra and DynamoDB default toward availability, with consistency adjustable per request. A single-leader relational database chooses consistency.

**Quorums.** With N replicas, a write acknowledged by W of them and a read that consults R of them will always overlap on at least one up-to-date replica if R + W > N. With N = 3, R = 2 and W = 2 gives consistent reads.

**Partitioning.** Hash partitioning spreads keys evenly but makes range scans expensive. Range partitioning keeps ranges together but concentrates hot ranges. **Consistent hashing** places nodes and keys on a ring, so adding a node moves only a small share of keys.

**Retries.** When a service recovers, thousands of clients retrying at once can knock it over again. Retry with **exponential backoff and jitter**: wait longer after each failure, with randomness so clients spread out.

**Recovery targets.** **RPO**, the recovery point objective, is how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time. **RTO**, the recovery time objective, is how long you can be down. A design for a region failure states both.

## 10.6 Seventeen designs

Each of these is a prompt from your bank. The skeletons below are the starting point for your one-page documents.

**1. Clickstream analytics: dashboards within 30 seconds, plus daily batch.** *(LinkedIn, Atlassian, Adobe, Amazon.)* Apps send events to an ingestion service that writes to Kafka, keyed by session. A stream processor de-duplicates on event id, sessionises, and aggregates per minute in update mode into a real-time store such as Pinot for dashboards. Raw events also land in an Iceberg bronze table; batch jobs build silver and gold daily, with late data merged within a lookback window. *Key decisions:* the freshness and finality split; partition count from throughput; event-time windows with watermarks. *Failure:* replay from Kafka with idempotent sinks; dead-letter topic for bad events.

**2. Change data capture into a warehouse.** *(LinkedIn, J.P. Morgan.)* Sprint 6. Log-based capture, Kafka keyed by primary key, raw changelog plus merged current state, version guard, reconciliation, slot-lag alerts.

**3. Real-time fraud detection.** *(Kotak Mahindra, Grab, Fanatics.)* A payment request calls a scoring service synchronously with a latency budget, say 100 milliseconds. The service reads precomputed features, such as transactions per card in the last 10 minutes and distance from the last location, from a low-latency store kept fresh by a stream processor over the transaction stream. A model scores; rules handle clear cases. If scoring times out, a fallback rule decides, because payments must not wait. Decisions and features are logged to the lake for training and audit. *Key decisions:* which features need streaming freshness and which can be batch; fail-open or fail-closed; the feedback loop from chargebacks, which arrive weeks later.

**4. Average speed of all cars in a city, in real time.** *(Grab; similar at Uber and Google.)* Drivers' phones send location pings every few seconds to Kafka, keyed by driver. A stream processor computes speed from consecutive pings per driver, which needs per-driver state, discards impossible values, then aggregates per city or per geospatial cell in sliding windows. Results go to a real-time store and a dashboard; raw pings go to the lake. *Key decisions:* geospatial indexing such as hexagonal cells; out-of-order pings; GPS noise; very high write volume.

**5. IoT telemetry at one message per second per device.** *(Google, Nvidia, PayPal.)* Devices connect through an ingestion gateway or managed IoT service into a stream. Hot path: a stream processor computes per-device health and raises anomaly alerts. Cold path: raw data lands in partitioned, compacted tables for analysis, with retention and downsampling, keeping full resolution for 30 days and minute averages for longer. *Key decisions:* time-series storage cost; device clock skew; late uploads after a device reconnects; alert fatigue.

**6. A central log analytics platform.** *(Cloudflare and Pinterest designs; your own bootcamp project 3.)* Agents such as Fluent Bit ship logs to Kafka. A stream job parses, adds metadata and writes Iceberg tables partitioned by day and service, compacted hourly. Trino or a log search engine serves queries. Sensitive fields are masked at ingest. Retention is enforced per service. *Key decisions:* small files, schema per service, cost per GB, access control for security logs.

**7. A task scheduler.** *(Fanatics.)* You answered this in sprint 9: a state store, a scheduling loop that releases tasks whose dependencies succeeded, workers with heartbeats, retries with backoff, and partitioned schedulers at scale.

**8. An executive metrics layer.** *(Uber: "an executive dashboard for business leaders".)* The problem is consistency, not speed. Gold marts from sprint 4, with metric definitions in one place, a semantic layer or metrics store, so every dashboard computes "completed trips" the same way. Pre-aggregate daily and weekly tables for speed. Certify the core metrics with tests and owners. *Key decisions:* definitions and ownership, freshness by 07:00, reconciliation with finance, and access by role.

**9. Ads impression and click attribution at billions a day.** *(Meta, Google, Uber Ads.)* 50 billion impressions and 500 million clicks a day: about 600,000 impressions a second on average. Impressions and clicks go to separate Kafka topics keyed by impression id. A stream job joins each click to its impression within a window (impressions arrive first; keep a day of impression state, keyed and compacted), and conversions from advertisers arrive up to 7 days later, so last-click attribution runs as a daily batch over a 7-day lookback and restates the affected days. Billing uses the batch numbers; dashboards use the stream. *Key decisions:* state size (600,000 a second × a day × 100 bytes is about 5 TB of join state, so keep only clicked impressions' keys, or join in batch); deduplicating click fraud; restating spend.

**10. An experimentation platform.** *(Meta, Uber, Airbnb.)* Thousands of experiments at once over billions of exposure events a day. Assignment is a hash of user and experiment id, so no lookup is needed; exposures are logged when the user actually sees the change. A daily batch joins exposures to metric events per user, computes per-user totals, then per-variant means and variances (CUPED to cut variance), and writes results with confidence intervals. *Key decisions:* the unit of randomisation; sample ratio mismatch checks; pre-computing per-user metric tables once and reusing them for every experiment, because joining raw events per experiment does not scale; guardrail metrics on every test.

**11. Deleting a user everywhere (GDPR) across a petabyte lake.** *(Any company with EU users.)* A deletion request must remove a person from every table within 30 days. Keep a deletion log keyed by user id. Tables are Iceberg or Delta, partitioned by date but with user id in every row, so a weekly job runs a merge-on-read delete for the batch of ids (deletion vectors or position deletes), then compaction rewrites the affected files and snapshot expiry removes the old ones, or the data is still there. Kafka topics hold at most 7 days, so they expire on their own; backups follow the same log. *Key decisions:* where personal data may live (a short list of tables with user id); batching deletes so you rewrite files once a week, not per request; proof of deletion for auditors. Cost: rewriting 1% of a 1 PB lake is 10 TB of reads and writes a week.

**12. A lineage and catalog platform.** *(Salesforce, LinkedIn's DataHub, Uber's Databook.)* Every job emits OpenLineage events (inputs, outputs, columns, run status) to Kafka; a service builds a graph of datasets and jobs, and a search index serves "who uses this column?". At 50,000 tables and 10,000 jobs a day, the graph is small; the hard parts are coverage (every engine must emit) and trust (owners, descriptions and tests). *Key decisions:* push (jobs emit) over pull (crawl query logs); column-level lineage for impact analysis before a schema change; ownership required to publish a table.

**13. Top-K and heavy hitters over a sliding window ("trending now").** *(X and Twitter trends, YouTube and TikTok trending, Amazon best sellers.)* 150,000 hashtags a second. Do not write a counter per event: each stream task keeps a **count-min sketch** (a grid of counters, d rows by w columns; each tag adds 1 in one column per row, chosen by d hash functions, and its estimate is the smallest of those d counters) plus a small heap of its own top candidates, and every second sends only those partial counts, keyed by window, to a second stage that merges them into the global top K. With ε = 0.001 and δ = 0.001, w = e/ε ≈ 2,700 and d = ln(1/δ) ≈ 7: about 19,000 counters, 76 KB per window, however many distinct tags there are. A sliding hour is 60 one-minute buckets: sketches add up, so the hour is the sum of the last 60 and the oldest bucket simply drops out. *Key decisions:* approximate is right for trending and wrong for billing (a sketch only overcounts, so keep exact daily counts in batch for the record); pre-aggregate before the shuffle, because heavy hitters are exactly the hot keys that overload one partition; filter bots and spam before counting; Space-Saving is the alternative when you need the top K with fixed memory and error bounds per item. *Practise it:* simulator challenge "Trending now".

**14. A metrics and time-series platform.** *(Datadog, Uber's M3, Netflix Atlas, Prometheus with Mimir or VictoriaMetrics.)* Agents on 5,000 hosts send 100 series every 10 seconds: 50,000 samples a second, ten times that at 1,000 series a host. Collectors write to Kafka, keyed by series id, so a database restart becomes lag instead of lost samples; ingesters write to a time-series store sharded by series. Gorilla-style compression (delta-of-delta timestamps, XOR of float values) stores a sample in about 1.4 bytes, so 50,000 a second is about 6 GB a day. Rollups keep 10-second points for 15 days, 1-minute for 90 days and 1-hour for years, and a query reads the coarsest resolution that fits its range. Alerts evaluate on the stream, on their own path, so a slow dashboard never delays a page. *Key decisions:* **cardinality** is the cost (series = metric × every combination of labels, so a user id or request id label multiplies it by millions: enforce limits per team at ingest); pull (Prometheus scrapes) or push (agents send); retention tiers; per-tenant quotas. *Practise it:* simulator challenge "Metrics platform".

**15. A web crawler.** *(Google, Common Crawl; now asked at companies collecting AI training data.)* A billion pages a month is about 385 pages a second; at 100 KB a page that is 100 TB a month raw, about 20 TB compressed, stored as WARC files in object storage with a metadata table (URL, fetch time, status, content hash). The URL frontier has priority queues (important and fast-changing pages first) in front of per-host queues, so each host is fetched at most once every few seconds, after reading and caching its robots.txt. Fetchers are asynchronous with a local DNS cache. Seen URLs live in a Bloom filter or a key-value set; page content is deduplicated by hash, and near-duplicates by SimHash. Parsed links go back to the frontier; recrawl follows each page's change rate. *Key decisions:* politeness and robots.txt; crawler traps (endless calendars and session ids) caught by depth and URL-pattern limits; freshness against coverage; for training data, licences, personal-data filtering and quality filters before anything is used.

**16. A Snowflake-style ID generator.** *(Twitter Snowflake, Discord, Instagram's sharded IDs.)* A 64-bit ID: 1 sign bit, 41 bits of milliseconds since a custom epoch (about 69 years), 10 bits of worker id (1,024 workers) and 12 bits of sequence (4,096 per millisecond per worker, about 4 million a second). IDs sort by time, fit a BIGINT, and need no coordination per ID; worker ids come from configuration or a lease in ZooKeeper or etcd. If the clock moves backwards (NTP), the worker waits until it passes the last timestamp it used, or refuses, rather than issue duplicates. *Key decisions:* time-ordered IDs keep B-tree inserts at the end of the index, while random UUIDv4 inserts scatter across it; UUIDv7 (RFC 9562, 2024) gives the same time order in a standard 128-bit form; a database sequence or ticket server is simpler but central; an ID reveals when it was made and roughly how many there are.

**17. A payments ledger.** *(Stripe, Square, Uber, any bank.)* Double-entry bookkeeping: every transaction is a set of entries whose debits equal its credits, appended to a journal that is never updated or deleted (a correction is a reversing entry). Balances are the sum of entries, kept as a materialised balance with a version number for fast reads. Amounts are integers in minor units, with a currency per account. Every API call carries an **idempotency key**, so a retried request posts once; a payment moves through a state machine (created, authorised, captured, settled, refunded), and calls to the card processor go through an outbox so the ledger and the outside world agree. Each day the ledger is reconciled against the processor's and the bank's settlement files, and every difference is a ticket. At 10,000 payments a second at peak with about 4 entries each, that is 40,000 rows a second, sharded by account in a strongly consistent store. *Key decisions:* consistency over availability (a payment may wait; it may not post twice); a hot merchant account split into sub-accounts that roll up; CDC from the ledger into the lake for finance and audit, never writes from the lake back; retention of at least 7 years.

## 10.7 AI in the design round

Interviewers now add AI to general design questions. Two kinds of follow-up are common.

**"Add an AI feature to this system."** Treat the model as one more component with its own requirements.

- **Context:** what data it needs, and through which tools or retrieval.
- **Evaluation:** how you measure quality before and after each change.
- **Budgets:** latency and cost per request, and what happens when they are exceeded.
- **Guardrails:** what it can read, what it can change, and where a person approves.
- **Observability:** a trace of every request with each model call, tool call, tokens and latency, so a bad answer can be explained.

**"Would you use several agents?"** One agent with good tools is the default. It is cheaper, faster and easier to debug. Several agents help when a task splits into **independent** subtasks that can run in parallel or need separate contexts, such as one agent reading logs while another reads recent code changes, with an orchestrator combining their findings. Each extra agent adds tokens, latency and places for errors to hide, so the split must earn its cost on your evaluation set.

**Reviewing AI-written code.** A common new interview task gives you a pipeline written by an assistant and asks what is wrong. The code usually runs. The flaws are in behaviour over time. In one published example, an assistant produced a working sentiment pipeline in about two minutes that overwrote the full history on every run, re-scored records that had not changed, and averaged over all of history when the business wanted a recent window. Read generated code with these questions:

1. What happens on the second run, and the hundredth?
2. Does it process only what changed?
3. Does it compute what the business asked for, at the right grain and window?
4. What does it cost per run as data grows?
5. What happens with duplicates, late data and nulls?

### Four AI designs, worked

AI-engineer loops now have a design round of their own. The method from 10.2 still applies: requirements, an estimate, the flow, then depth where the interviewer points. What changes is the arithmetic (tokens, not just requests), the budgets (time to first token, cost per request) and the failure modes (rate limits, wrong answers, actions that cannot be undone). Prices below are assumptions for the arithmetic, not quotes; say yours out loud.

**A. RAG over 10 million documents at 1,000 questions a second, with a latency budget.**

*Estimate.* 10 million documents of about 2,000 tokens, cut into chunks of about 500 tokens, is about 40 million chunks. At 1,024 dimensions in float32 a vector is 4 KB, so the index is about 160 GB, or about 40 GB quantised to int8, plus graph overhead: shard it (four shards of 10 million chunks) and replicate each shard for search throughput. 1,000 questions a second is 86 million a day; at about 2,000 input and 300 output tokens each, that is about 170 billion input tokens a day, so at an assumed $0.20 per million input tokens, input alone is about $35,000 a day before caching. That number drives the design.

*Flow.* Gateway (identity, quotas) → embed the question → hybrid retrieval (vector search plus keyword search, merged by rank fusion), **filtered by the user's permissions**, which are stored with each chunk → rerank the top 50 with a cross-encoder → build the prompt with numbered passages → the model answers with citations, streamed. A semantic cache sits before the model: questions whose embedding is very close to one already answered get the stored answer.

*Latency budget (p95, to the first token).* Gateway 10 ms, embedding 20 ms, retrieval 50 ms, reranking 100 ms, prompt 10 ms, model time to first token 500 to 800 ms: about 1 to 1.5 seconds, then the answer streams. The model is the budget; everything else must stay small.

*Decisions to defend.* The cache (hit rates of 30 to 60% on support-style traffic) and routing easy questions to a small model are the two cost levers; prompt caching of the fixed system prompt is a third. Freshness: documents change through CDC or events, and only changed chunks are re-embedded. Wrong answers: answer only from the retrieved passages, cite them, and say "I do not know" below a retrieval score; measure groundedness on a golden set before every change. *Failure:* the provider throttles (cache, a second provider through the gateway), a shard dies (replicas), the cache empties after a deploy (warm it, or have throughput for a cold cache).

Prove it: [Teach me this design](#/sim/teach/airag) and the challenge [RAG at 1,000 questions a second](#/sim/sc/ragqps).

**B. A support agent with tools and handoff to a person.**

*Requirements.* Answer customers in chat, look up orders, issue refunds and cancellations within policy, and hand the conversation to a person with the full history when it cannot help. Never refund twice; never refund above a limit without approval.

*Flow.* One agent (not several: the task does not split into independent parts) with a short list of tools. Read tools such as order status run inline. Write tools (refund, cancel) and the handoff are **jobs on a queue**, each with an idempotency key, so a retry never acts twice; refunds over the limit wait for a person's approval. The agent replies at once ("your refund is on its way") and the worker does the slow part. The person who takes a handoff sees the transcript and the agent's notes.

*Estimate.* 60 messages a second at peak, 2 to 4 model calls per turn, so 120 to 240 model calls a second. Budget: a reply within about 2.5 seconds.

*Decisions to defend.* Guardrails live in the tools, not the prompt: the refund tool itself enforces the limit and the customer's ownership of the order. Prompt injection: text from customers and tool results is data; the agent cannot call tools it was not given. Evals: a golden set of conversations scored on resolution, correct tool use and wrong-action rate, and a regression gate before every prompt or model change. Observability: one trace per turn with every model and tool call, tokens and latency.

Prove it: [Teach me this design](#/sim/teach/aiagent) and the challenge [Support agent: slow tools and handoff](#/sim/sc/agenttools).

**C. A company LLM gateway.**

*Requirements.* Every team calls models through one service. Teams never hold provider keys; each has a quota and a monthly budget; spend is reported per team; a provider outage does not stop the company.

*Flow.* Team key → gateway → policy checks (quota as a token bucket per team, budget, allowed models, PII redaction where required) → cache (exact, then semantic) → **router** (by task, or small model first and escalate when it is unsure) → provider, with retries, timeouts and failover to a second provider or region → a trace with tokens, cost, latency and team for every call. Batch work goes to the providers' batch APIs at a lower price.

*Estimate.* 300 requests a second at peak from three teams; if two thirds are short classification and extraction prompts, routing them to a model at a tenth of the price cuts the bill by about half or more.

*Decisions to defend.* Where quotas are enforced (the gateway, before the call), what happens at the limit (queue batch work, reject interactive work with a clear error), how model upgrades are rolled out (shadow traffic and evals first, then a percentage), and who owns it (a platform team, as a product with an SLA).

Prove it: [Teach me this design](#/sim/teach/aigateway) and the challenge [Company LLM gateway: route by task](#/sim/sc/llmgw).

**D. Document extraction at scale.**

*Requirements.* Extract fields (supplier, dates, line items, totals) from 13 million invoices a day, three times that at month-end, into a table finance can trust, within minutes.

*Flow.* Store each raw file keyed by its content hash (a re-sent file is not processed twice) → workers split pages and run OCR or layout parsing → **a queue** in front of the model → the model extracts into a fixed JSON schema with a confidence per field → validation (schema, totals that add up, dates that parse) → low-confidence or failing documents go to a person; their corrections become eval and fine-tuning data → the lake keeps raw file, fields, model and prompt version, and review outcome.

*Estimate.* 150 documents a second; a ten-page invoice is about 10,000 input tokens, so about 1.5 million tokens a second, which is why work that can wait goes to batch capacity at about half the price, and why a smaller fine-tuned model becomes worth it once labelled data exists.

*Decisions to defend.* The queue turns bursts into backlog instead of errors and keeps calls under the rate limit. Idempotency by content hash. Field-level evals (precision and recall per field on a labelled set) and a review rate target. Re-running a document after a prompt change touches only the documents whose prompt version is old.

Prove it: [Teach me this design](#/sim/teach/aiextract) and the challenge [Document extraction: survive month-end](#/sim/sc/extract).

**Part one: review (20 minutes).** An assistant was asked for "average sentiment per restaurant, updated daily, for the restaurant dashboard". It produced this, and it runs without errors. Find four problems before opening the answer.

```python
from pyspark.sql import functions as F

def run(spark, day):
    reviews = spark.read.table("silver.reviews")
    scored = reviews.withColumn("sentiment", score_with_llm(F.col("text")))
    daily = (scored.groupBy("restaurant_id")
                   .agg(F.avg("sentiment").alias("avg_sentiment")))
    daily.write.mode("overwrite").saveAsTable("gold.restaurant_sentiment")
```

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **It reads all history every day.** The `day` argument is never used. Cost and run time grow without limit.
2. **It re-scores every review on every run.** Reviews do not change, so each should be scored once and stored. With a model call per row, this is the expensive one.
3. **It averages over all time.** A dashboard of current sentiment needs a recent window, such as the last 30 days. A restaurant that improved last month still shows its old average.
4. **It overwrites the table.** Yesterday's values are lost, so trends cannot be shown and a bad run cannot be compared with a good one. Write one partition per day.

The fix: score only new reviews into a silver table with the score, prompt version and model; then compute a dated gold partition for a rolling window from stored scores.

</details>

TITLE: Weekly cohort retention
KIND: sql
MIN: 18
TAG: sql
SRC: LinkedIn, 5 YOE
PROBLEM:
Table `events(user_id, event_date)`.

A user's **cohort** is the week of their first event, where weeks start on Monday. For each cohort and each week after it in which members were active, return:

`cohort_week, week_number, active_users, retention_pct`

`week_number` is 0 for the cohort week itself. `retention_pct` is active users as a percentage of the cohort's size, rounded to one decimal. Order by cohort, then week.
SOLUTION:
```sql
WITH firsts AS (
  SELECT user_id,
         CAST(DATE_TRUNC('week', MIN(event_date)) AS DATE) AS cohort_week
  FROM events
  GROUP BY user_id
),
activity AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT e.user_id, f.cohort_week,
         CAST((CAST(DATE_TRUNC('week', e.event_date) AS DATE) - f.cohort_week) / 7 AS INTEGER) AS week_number
  FROM events e
  JOIN firsts f ON f.user_id = e.user_id
),
sizes AS (
  SELECT cohort_week, COUNT(*) AS cohort_size
  FROM firsts
  GROUP BY cohort_week
)
SELECT a.cohort_week,
       a.week_number,
       COUNT(*) AS active_users,
       ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) / s.cohort_size, 1) AS retention_pct
FROM activity a
JOIN sizes s ON s.cohort_week = a.cohort_week
GROUP BY a.cohort_week, a.week_number, s.cohort_size
ORDER BY a.cohort_week, a.week_number;
```

Three steps: assign each user a cohort, list the distinct weeks each user was active relative to their cohort, then count per cohort and week and divide by the cohort's size.

Two details. `DISTINCT` in `activity` is essential, or a user active on three days of one week counts three times. And week truncation returns a timestamp in several engines, so it is cast to a date before subtracting. Subtracting two dates gives days; dividing by 7 gives weeks.

LinkedIn followed up with a "cohort-size-adjusted" retention. Small cohorts produce noisy percentages, so when comparing cohorts, either weight each cohort's rate by its size, or require a minimum size before showing it.

KIND: sql

SRC: Google, 5 YOE
PROBLEM:
Table `events(user_id, event_type, event_ts)`. Event types include `add_to_cart` and `purchase`.

Return a single value, `conversion_pct`: of the users who added something to their cart at least once, the percentage who made a purchase **within seven days after any of their add-to-cart events**. Round to one decimal.
SOLUTION:
```sql
WITH carts AS (
  SELECT user_id, event_ts AS cart_ts
  FROM events
  WHERE event_type = 'add_to_cart'
),
converted AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT c.user_id
  FROM carts c
  JOIN events p
    ON p.user_id = c.user_id
   AND p.event_type = 'purchase'
   AND p.event_ts >= c.cart_ts
   AND p.event_ts <= c.cart_ts + INTERVAL '7' DAY
)
SELECT ROUND(100.0 * (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM converted)
                   / (SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) FROM carts), 1) AS conversion_pct;
```

The denominator is everyone who reached the first step of the funnel. The numerator is the subset who reached the second step within the time limit. Users who purchased without a cart event are outside the funnel, and are excluded from both.

The join on a time range is the important part. A purchase before the cart event, or more than seven days after it, does not count. A user with several cart events counts once, which is why the numerator uses `DISTINCT`.

TITLE: Trapping rain water
KIND: dsa
MIN: 15
TAG: dsa
SRC: PayPal, 3 YOE
PROBLEM:
Given a list of non-negative integers representing bar heights of width 1, return how much water is trapped between them after rain.

`[0,1,0,2,1,0,1,3,2,1,2,1]` returns 6.

PayPal asked for an answer optimal in both time and space.
SOLUTION:
```python
def trap(heights):
    left, right = 0, len(heights) - 1
    left_max = right_max = water = 0
    while left < right:
        if heights[left] < heights[right]:
            left_max = max(left_max, heights[left])
            water += left_max - heights[left]
            left += 1
        else:
            right_max = max(right_max, heights[right])
            water += right_max - heights[right]
            right -= 1
    return water
```

Water above a bar is the smaller of the tallest bar to its left and the tallest to its right, minus its own height. The straightforward solution precomputes both maxima in two arrays: O(n) time and O(n) space.

The two-pointer version removes the arrays. Move inwards from the side with the lower bar. If the left bar is lower than the right one, then somewhere on the right is a bar at least as tall, so the water at the left position depends only on `left_max`, which is already known. Time O(n), space O(1). Explain that reasoning; it is what the interviewer is checking.

TITLE: Too many accesses in five minutes, as a stream
KIND: python

TAG: streaming
SRC: Confluent, 5–6 YOE
PROBLEM:
Access events arrive one at a time, in timestamp order, as `(user, ts)` with `ts` in seconds.

Write a class `AccessMonitor(limit=3, window_seconds=300)` with a method `record(user, ts)` that returns `True` when this event means the user has accessed the system **more than `limit` times within the window**, counting events whose timestamps are at most `window_seconds` apart, and `False` otherwise.

Memory per user must stay bounded no matter how many events a user sends.
SOLUTION:
```python
from collections import deque

class AccessMonitor:
    def __init__(self, limit=3, window_seconds=300):
        self.limit = limit
        self.window = window_seconds
        self.recent = {}                      # user -> the last limit+1 timestamps

    def record(self, user, ts):
        q = self.recent.get(user)
        if q is None:
            q = self.recent[user] = deque(maxlen=self.limit + 1)
        q.append(ts)                          # the oldest falls off automatically
        return len(q) == self.limit + 1 and ts - q[0] <= self.window
```

"More than three in five minutes" means the current event and the three before it all fall inside the window. So only the last four timestamps per user matter, and a deque with a maximum length keeps exactly those. Each call is O(1), and memory per user is fixed. This is the same idea as `LAG(ts, 3)` in the SQL version of this problem from sprint 5.

Two follow-ups to raise yourself. Memory across users still grows with the number of users, so remove users with no events in the last window, as a stream processor's state timeout would. And if events can arrive out of order, insert in sorted position, or buffer briefly with a watermark.

Q: Design a system to calculate the average speed of all cars in a city in real time.
SRC: Grab, 8 YOE
TAG: design
A: Cars send GPS pings every few seconds to an ingestion service, which writes to Kafka or Kinesis keyed by vehicle, so each vehicle's pings stay in order. A stream processor keeps the last ping per vehicle in state, computes speed from distance and time between consecutive pings, and drops impossible values from GPS noise. It aggregates speed per city, and per geospatial cell for maps, in sliding windows by event time with a short watermark. Aggregates go to a real-time store for dashboards and to Redshift, Snowflake or BigQuery for history. Raw pings land in the lake. At scale, partitions follow vehicle count, and state is bounded by evicting inactive vehicles.

Q: Design a pipeline that ingests real-time stock market data and makes it available for analytics within seconds.
SRC: Google, 5 YOE
TAG: design
A: Market feed handlers publish ticks to Pub/Sub or Kafka, keyed by symbol for ordering. A stream processor, Dataflow on Google Cloud, normalises ticks, de-duplicates by sequence number, and computes windowed aggregates such as one-second bars and moving averages by event time with a small allowed lateness. Results stream into BigQuery, partitioned by date and clustered by symbol, for analytics within seconds, and into a low-latency cache for live screens. Raw ticks are archived for replay and compliance. I would discuss the batch versus real-time trade-off for heavy end-of-day analytics, the handling of feed gaps and corrections, and scaling around market open.

Q: IoT devices send data every second. Design a real-time ingestion pipeline into BigQuery.
SRC: Google, 5 YOE
TAG: design
A: Devices publish to Pub/Sub, through an IoT gateway if they need it, with device id as an attribute for ordering. Dataflow, Apache Beam, validates and parses messages, routes malformed ones to a dead-letter topic, handles late data with event-time windows and allowed lateness, and writes raw rows to BigQuery tables partitioned by date or hour and clustered by device id, plus windowed aggregates for dashboards. Challenges: ordering per device, late uploads after reconnection, device clock skew, and cost. For cost, I would use the storage write API, keep raw data partitioned with expiry, and downsample old data into aggregate tables.

Q: How would you collect logs and metrics from thousands of GPU servers, and alert on anomalies?
SRC: Nvidia, 5–7 YOE
TAG: design
A: An agent on each server collects metrics such as temperature, utilisation, memory errors and power, and ships logs. Metrics go to a time-series system with high write capacity; logs go through Kafka into a log store and the lake. Alerting has two layers: threshold rules for known failure signals, such as temperature or error counts, and anomaly detection against each server's own baseline and its peers in the same cluster, to catch drift. Alerts are grouped by rack or cluster to avoid storms, and routed with context. History in the lake supports failure prediction. At scale, I partition by cluster, downsample old metrics and monitor the monitoring system itself.

Q: Design an ETL pipeline for ad click data, over a million records a day, with raw logs in S3 or GCS. Cover bronze, silver and gold, late data, de-duplication and schema.
SRC: Atlassian, 5 YOE
TAG: design
A: **Bronze:** raw logs ingested incrementally, unchanged, with load metadata, partitioned by arrival date. **Silver:** parsed and typed, de-duplicated on click id with `ROW_NUMBER` keeping the first occurrence, bot traffic filtered, joined to campaign dimensions, partitioned by event date. Late data handled by reprocessing a lookback window and merging on click id, or in streaming with a watermark. **Gold:** impressions and clicks as separate fact tables at their own grain, joined in aggregates for click-through rates by campaign and day, in a star schema with campaign, ad, date and placement dimensions. Monitor volume and duplicate rates; fix skew on large campaigns with salting or adaptive execution.

SRC: Uber, 5 YOE

Q: Design a scalable recommendation system for e-commerce using real-time data.
SRC: Stripe, 4 YOE
TAG: design
A: **Ingestion:** user events through Kafka. **Features:** a stream processor computes recent behaviour such as last viewed categories and session intent into the online part of a feature store, a low-latency key-value store; batch jobs compute long-term features and train models offline from the lake, writing to the same feature definitions so training and serving match. **Serving:** a service retrieves candidates, for example from embeddings or popularity, ranks them with a model using online features, and returns results within tens of milliseconds, with caching for popular items. **Feedback:** impressions and clicks are logged back to the lake for training and A/B tests. Key risks: training and serving skew, and cold start.

Q: How would you design for billions of records? How do you decide which big data tools to use?
SRC: Stripe, 4 YOE
TAG: design
A: Start from access patterns and requirements, not tools. Billions of records mean partitioning everything: storage by time and a clustering key, processing in parallel by key, serving by the key users query. I choose tools by fit. Object storage with a table format for durable, cheap storage. Spark for large batch transformation. Kafka with Flink or Structured Streaming when freshness is minutes or seconds. A warehouse for analytical SQL. A key-value store such as DynamoDB for millisecond lookups. I also weigh the team's skills, operational cost and what already exists. I prefer the smallest set of tools that meets the requirements.

Q: How would you store terabytes or petabytes of data? Compare HDFS, S3 and DynamoDB, and explain fault tolerance and fast retrieval.
SRC: Amazon, 4 YOE
TAG: design
A: For bulk analytical data, S3 with columnar files and a table format: cheap, durable and independent of compute. HDFS where compute must sit with data on-premises. DynamoDB is not bulk storage; it serves key lookups in milliseconds, so it holds the serving view, not history. Fault tolerance: S3 stores objects redundantly across facilities; HDFS replicates blocks three times; DynamoDB replicates across availability zones. Fast retrieval: partitioning and statistics in the lake, an index or key design in the serving store, and a cache in front of hot keys. At ten times the size, the design holds; partitions, compaction and serving capacity are what scale.

Q: Explain the CAP theorem and how it applies to databases such as DynamoDB or Cassandra.
SRC: Wipro, 2 YOE
TAG: design
A: In a distributed system, when a network partition prevents nodes from communicating, the system must choose: remain consistent by refusing some operations, or remain available by serving possibly stale data. Without a partition, both are possible. Cassandra and DynamoDB are designed for availability, with replication and eventual consistency by default, but both let you tune per request: Cassandra with consistency levels such as quorum, and DynamoDB with strongly consistent reads. The practical point is that consistency is a per-operation choice with a latency cost, chosen by what the data is used for.

Q: What are the advantages of Cassandra over relational databases, and what are its common uses?
SRC: Cisco, 4 YOE
TAG: design
A: Cassandra scales writes horizontally with no single leader: every node accepts writes, data is partitioned by key across a ring and replicated, and adding nodes adds capacity. It stays available through node and data-centre failures, and its log-structured storage makes writes very fast. The costs: no joins, limited ad hoc queries, and data modelled per query, often duplicated. It suits high-volume writes read by known keys: time-series data from sensors, event logs, user activity feeds, messaging, and the latest state per device. It is a poor fit for analytics or for transactions that span many entities.

Q: What caching strategies would you use for frequently read but rarely updated data?
SRC: Fanatics, 6 YOE
TAG: design
A: Cache-aside is the default: the application reads the cache, and on a miss reads the source and fills the cache with a time to live. For data that changes rarely, a long time to live with explicit invalidation on update works well, triggered by the write path or by change data capture. Read-through and write-through caches move that logic into the cache layer. For very hot items, a local in-process cache in front of a shared one such as Redis cuts network calls. I watch for stampedes when a hot key expires, solved with request coalescing or early refresh, and I monitor hit rate.

Q: Design a booking system for a three-seat theatre that accepts only cash. How do you keep bookings correct?
SRC: Google, 5 YOE
TAG: design
A: Tables: shows, seats per show, and bookings with show, seat, customer, status and timestamps. The constraint that matters is that a seat can be booked once per show, enforced by a unique constraint on show and seat, not by application checks, because two clerks can try at the same moment. Booking is one transaction: insert the booking, and if the unique constraint fails, the seat is taken. Cash means payment is recorded at the counter, so a booking moves from held to paid, with holds expiring after a few minutes. With three seats, the interviewer is testing whether you think about concurrency and constraints, not scale.

Q: If you had to build a data product for half the infrastructure cost, what trade-offs would you make?
SRC: Google, 5 YOE
TAG: design
A: First find where the cost is, since most of it usually sits in a few places. Then trade along explicit axes. Freshness: hourly instead of streaming where decisions allow it. Retention: full detail for a shorter period, aggregates after that. Precision: approximate distinct counts and percentiles. Compute: serverless and auto-suspend, Spot capacity for batch. Storage layout: partitioning and compaction to cut scans. Scope: retire unused tables and dashboards. I would agree each trade-off with the users, measure the saving, and keep the core metrics exact.

Q: When is a multi-agent design worth it, and how would you add an AI feature to a data platform responsibly?
TAG: ai
A: A single agent with well-designed tools is the default. Multiple agents are worth their cost when the work splits into independent parts that benefit from parallel execution or separate context, and the split improves results on an evaluation set. For any AI feature I specify five things like any other component: the context and tools it uses; an evaluation set and metric; latency and cost budgets with behaviour when exceeded; guardrails on what it can read and change, with human approval for changes; and tracing of every model and tool call with tokens and latency. Then a staged rollout, starting in shadow mode.

Q: Product demands real-time reporting, but the infrastructure budget is limited. How do you negotiate the trade-off?
SRC: Flipkart, 4 YOE
TAG: behavioral
A: Turn "real-time" into a requirement with a number: which metrics, how fresh, and what decision changes with fresher data. Usually only a few metrics need minutes, and the rest are fine hourly or daily. Then present options with cost: hourly batch, micro-batch every few minutes, true streaming, each with an estimated monthly cost and what it enables. Recommend the cheapest option that meets the actual decision cadence, often near-real-time for a handful of metrics only. Agree it in writing, with a review date. Use a real example from your work if you have one, with the outcome.

## 11.1 Why this sprint exists

Half of every loop in your bank is not technical. There is a hiring-manager round, a behavioral round, and often a product or case round: Meta measures engagement for a new feature, Atlassian asks why click-through rose while conversions fell, Google asks you to sell its cloud to a non-technical buyer. Candidates who prepare only for the technical rounds lose offers here.

The same skills define a role that is growing quickly. A **forward deployed engineer** works inside a customer's environment, finds out what they need, and ships production software for them. Postings grew from 643 in April 2025 to 5,330 in April 2026, according to Indeed data reported by Business Insider. In an analysis of 1,000 postings, the most requested skills were Python, AI agents, TypeScript, AWS and LLMs, with SQL and a modern data stack assumed. When Scaler checked Indian openings, Google Cloud, Sarvam AI and Accenture were hiring for such roles; Palantir, OpenAI and Anthropic had none in India at the time.

Your profile fits. Years of analytical work with business teams, then data engineering, then agentic automation for a go-to-market team. This sprint trains the part of that profile that interviews test least directly and value most.

## 11.2 Discovery

Every engagement begins with a conversation in which the customer describes a symptom and you find the problem. Go in with a list, and leave with written answers.

- **Who** will use what you build, and how often?
- **What decision** will it change? If nobody can name one, stop and ask again.
- **What does done look like?** Ask them to describe the screen or the number they want to see.
- **What do they do today?** The current workaround shows what matters and what data exists.
- **What data is there**, who owns it, and how good is it?
- **What constraints** apply: security, deadlines, budget, systems you may not touch?
- **How will we know it worked?** Agree a measure before you build.

Then restate what you heard in two sentences and ask if it is right. Customers often correct the restatement, which is the most useful moment of the meeting.

## 11.3 Decomposition

The signature forward deployed round gives you a vague prompt and watches how you break it down. Interview guides report it as the most heavily weighted round, and the one most candidates fail, usually by drawing an architecture in the first two minutes.

A method, worked through on a real prompt from those guides: *"A logistics company wants to know why deliveries are running late. Where do you start?"*

**1. Clarify the goal and the user.** Who is asking? An operations director who wants fewer late deliveries next quarter. What will they do with the answer? Change staffing, routes or promises.

**2. Define the measure precisely.** "Late" against what? The time promised to the end customer at checkout, delivered more than 15 minutes after that. Measured per delivery, reported as a rate. Write it down, because half of the later disagreement will be about this sentence.

**3. Break the outcome into parts.** A delivery passes through stages, and lateness accumulates in them:

```
order placed → assigned to a driver → picked up → in transit → delivered
     (wait)          (wait)              (drive)       (last mile)
```

Late deliveries can now be attributed: what share of the delay happened in each stage?

**4. Form hypotheses, ranked.** Not enough drivers at peak times, so assignment waits. Warehouses slow at specific sites, so pickup waits. Bad promises: the promised time ignores distance or traffic. Specific areas with hard last-mile access. Rank by likely impact and by how easy each is to check.

**5. Map data to hypotheses.** Stage timestamps from the order system, driver shifts, warehouse throughput, delivery addresses. Note which exist, which are trustworthy, and which are missing.

**6. Propose the smallest valuable deliverable.** Not a platform. A table of every delivery with the time spent in each stage, and one chart of late rate by stage, site and hour, within a few days. It will show which hypothesis to pursue.

**7. Say how you will know it worked.** The late rate in the next month, compared with the same period before, for the changes made because of the analysis.

Throughout, think aloud, ask before assuming, and invite the interviewer to push back. When they do, adjust openly. The round is scored on the quality of your thinking and on how you take a challenge, not on finding a single answer.

## 11.4 The smallest valuable build

Engineers tend to build the general solution. Customers need the specific one first. The smallest valuable build serves **one user**, for **one decision**, on **real data**, and can be shown within days. Everything else waits until that user has used it.

Three rules help:

- **Real data early.** A demo on synthetic data proves nothing about the customer's mess. Their timestamps will be in three time zones, and finding that on day one is worth more than any slide.
- **Show, then ask.** A rough chart on their data starts a better conversation than a requirements document.
- **Write down what you did not build** and why. It prevents scope arguments and shows judgment.

## 11.5 Product metrics and experiments

Product rounds test whether you understand what the data is for.

**Activity.** Daily, weekly and monthly active users. **Stickiness**, DAU divided by MAU, shows how often monthly users return. **Retention** by cohort, which you built in sprint 10. **Funnels**: the share of users who pass each step, such as impression, click, add to cart, purchase.

**Choosing metrics.** A **north star** metric captures value to customers, such as completed trips per week. **Guardrail** metrics must not get worse while you push it, such as cancellation rate and support tickets. A team that optimises clicks without a guardrail on purchases gets more clicks and less revenue.

**Reading a metric change.** Atlassian's question: after a new recommendation algorithm, click-through rose 10% and conversion fell 5%. Work through it:

- Check the definitions: is conversion per click or per impression? If per click, more low-intent clicks will lower it even when total purchases rise. Compute **purchases per impression**, which is the number the business cares about.
- Segment: by user type, product, device and position. A change that helps one segment can hurt another.
- Look at what users do after clicking: bounce rate, time on page, how relevant the clicked item was.
- Check the experiment itself: was the split clean?

**A/B testing essentials.**

- Choose the randomisation unit to match the effect. Usually the user. For marketplace features where users affect each other, such as driver matching in a city, randomise by city or time window.
- Fix the primary metric, guardrails and sample size before starting, from the smallest effect worth detecting.
- Run for at least one full weekly cycle, and do not stop early because the result looks significant. Checking repeatedly and stopping at the first good result inflates false positives.
- Check the **sample ratio**. If a planned 50/50 split shows 53/47 on a large sample, something in assignment or logging is broken, and the result cannot be trusted.

## 11.6 Stories, and your own story

Behavioral rounds are scored on evidence. **STAR** is the structure: the **situation** in two sentences, the **task** you owned, the **actions** you took, which is most of the answer, and the **result** with a number, followed by what you learned.

Prepare a **story bank** of eight to ten stories, each tagged with the themes it can answer: ownership, conflict, failure, ambiguity, influencing without authority, tight deadline, mentoring, a stakeholder disagreement, learning something fast, a customer focus. One good story can answer three questions. Map your stories to each company's stated values before its loop: Amazon's leadership principles, Google's emphasis on collaboration and humility, Atlassian's openness.

**Your career story.** Interviewers will notice twelve years of experience with four and a half years titled data engineer. Address it directly and briefly, as a choice, not an apology:

> "I spent my first years on the analytical side of data at Google, working with the people who used the data. I moved into data engineering because I kept seeing that the pipelines decided what was possible. That background is why I design pipelines around how the data will be used, and it is why I ended up building the agentic workflows at Salesforce for a go-to-market team."

Then move to your strongest pipeline with numbers.

**Your resume.** Lead each role with the biggest pipeline and its scale. Put numbers in every bullet you can defend in detail; you will be asked about each one. Keep AI as a clear differentiator, after the pipelines. Add the program's platform repository and the on-call agent as a project, with a link.

## 11.7 Code-reading and AI-assisted rounds

Two newer formats need specific practice.

**Code comprehension.** Prep publishers report that Apple runs a round where you read an existing pipeline, explain what it does, find where it breaks, and propose a refactor, without writing new code. Read top-down: entry point, inputs, outputs, then each transformation. Say aloud what each block does before judging it. Then apply the review questions from sprint 10: reruns, incrementality, correctness of intent, cost, edge cases.

**AI-enabled coding.** Meta began rolling out a round in October 2025, for software engineering roles, where an assistant is available in a chat panel beside a multi-file codebase. It runs about an hour in three phases: fix a bug, implement a feature, then optimise against harder tests. You write the code; the assistant can only advise. It is graded on problem solving, code quality, how well you understand and verify what the assistant suggests, and communication. Candidates who leaned on the assistant without showing understanding were marked down.

Company policies differ widely. Prep sites report that Amazon treats undisclosed AI use as cheating, while Meta, Google and Canva have rounds where AI is expected. **Ask each recruiter** which applies to each round. Practise both ways.

How to use the assistant well when it is allowed:

1. Read the code yourself first. Ask the assistant to explain parts you do not understand, not to solve the task.
2. Write specific prompts: the function, the constraint, the input shape.
3. Read every line it suggests before you type it, and say why you accept or change it.
4. Run tests often. Add one edge-case test of your own.
5. Narrate. The interviewer is grading your judgment, which they can only hear.

## Build: a one-day customer build (2.25 hours)

Paste this lab into a chat with Claude and ask it to **generate the DE 11 starter kit**. The starter kit contains a fictional customer, **Swiftline Logistics**, with a one-page brief from their operations director and four messy CSV files: orders, driver assignments, pickups and deliveries. The data has the problems real customers have: timestamps in mixed time zones, duplicated status events, missing promised times for some orders, and a city code that changed spelling in the middle of the month.

Work in four timed blocks. Stop each one when its time is up.

1. **Discovery, 25 minutes.** Read the brief. Write `discovery.md`: your questions from section 11.2, the answers you can infer from the brief, the assumptions you are making, and the definition of "late" you will use.
2. **Build, 60 minutes.** A small pipeline, in Spark, dbt or plain SQL, that produces one row per delivery with the time spent in each stage and a late flag. Write down every data problem you fixed and how.
3. **Show, 25 minutes.** One chart: late rate by stage, site or hour, whichever is most revealing. One table of the five worst sites or hours.
4. **Memo, 15 minutes.** One page for the director: three findings with numbers, one recommended next step, and what you did not do.

Then ask Claude to **play the Swiftline director**. Present your memo in five minutes and answer questions for ten.

**Done when** all four files exist and you have presented them. Record what the "director" challenged you on.

## Incident: "your dashboard is wrong" (30 minutes)

The director replies: *"Your late rate is 18%. Our operations report says 11%. Your dashboard is wrong."* They cannot say more. The starter kit includes their report as a CSV.

1. Get specifics first: which period, which sites, which number. Find five deliveries that are late in your data and on time in theirs.
2. Compare definitions using those five. You will find that their report measures lateness from dispatch time, not from the time promised to the customer, and excludes failed delivery attempts.
3. Write a three-sentence explanation the director can forward to their CEO, with both numbers and what each measures.
4. Propose which definition to use going forward, and why, and offer to show both until they decide.

Neither number was wrong. Being able to say that calmly, with five examples, is what customers remember.

## AI lab: AI-assisted coding round practice (1 hour)

1. **With the assistant, 35 minutes.** Ask Claude for an AI-enabled practice task: a small multi-file Python repository from your platform with one bug, one feature to add and tiered tests. Set your assistant to chat only, so it can advise but not edit files. Work through the three phases. Keep a log of each prompt you sent and whether you accepted, changed or rejected the answer.
2. **Without the assistant, 20 minutes.** A comparable single problem with no AI at all.
3. **Review, 5 minutes.** Read your log. Mark prompts that asked for an answer instead of help. Note one suggestion you caught being wrong, or one you should have checked and did not.

**Done when** both tasks are attempted and the log is reviewed. The goal is two sets of reflexes, because you will not always know in advance which round you are in.

TITLE: Daily active users and their seven-day average
KIND: sql
MIN: 12
TAG: sql
SRC: Meta, 3 YOE
PROBLEM:
Table `events(user_id, event_ts)`. Every day in the range has at least one event.

Return `day, dau, dau_7d_avg` for each day, where `dau` is the number of distinct users that day and `dau_7d_avg` is the average DAU over that day and the six days before it, rounded to one decimal. For the first six days, average over the days available. Order by day.
SOLUTION:
```sql
WITH dau AS (
  SELECT CAST(event_ts AS DATE) AS day,
         COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) AS dau
  FROM events
  GROUP BY CAST(event_ts AS DATE)
)
SELECT day,
       dau,
       ROUND(AVG(dau) OVER (ORDER BY day ROWS BETWEEN 6 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW), 1) AS dau_7d_avg
FROM dau
ORDER BY day;
```

Aggregate to one row per day first, then apply the window to the daily numbers. Averaging over raw events would weight busy users more heavily.

`ROWS BETWEEN 6 PRECEDING` counts rows, so it equals seven calendar days only when no day is missing. The problem guarantees that. In real data, join to a calendar table first so empty days appear with a DAU of zero.

Meta's follow-up was how to detect missing or duplicated data in such a dashboard. Compare daily counts with the source and with the same weekday in previous weeks, and check for gaps in the calendar.

TITLE: Reacted but never commented in the same week
KIND: sql
MIN: 13
TAG: sql
SRC: Meta, 3 YOE
PROBLEM:
Table `events(user_id, action, event_ts)`, where `action` is `reaction` or `comment`. Weeks start on Monday.

Return each `user_id, week_start` where the user made at least one reaction during that week and **no comment during the same week**. Order by user, then week.
SOLUTION:
```sql
WITH wk AS (
  SELECT user_id, action,
         CAST(DATE_TRUNC('week', event_ts) AS DATE) AS week_start
  FROM events
)
SELECT DISTINCT r.user_id, r.week_start
FROM wk r
WHERE r.action = 'reaction'
  AND NOT EXISTS (
    SELECT 1
    FROM wk c
    WHERE c.user_id = r.user_id
      AND c.week_start = r.week_start
      AND c.action = 'comment'
  )
ORDER BY r.user_id, r.week_start;
```

An anti-join scoped to the same user and the same week. Truncating both sides to the week first is what makes "the same week" a simple equality.

Conditional aggregation works too: group by user and week, and keep groups with `SUM(CASE WHEN action = 'reaction' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) > 0` and `SUM(CASE WHEN action = 'comment' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) = 0`. It reads the table once, which is better on very large data. Know both.

TITLE: Next closest time from the same digits
KIND: dsa
MIN: 15
TAG: dsa
SRC: Google, 5 YOE
PROBLEM:
Given a time `"HH:MM"`, return the next time, moving forward and wrapping past midnight, that uses **only digits already present** in the given time. Digits may be reused any number of times. If no other time works, return the same time.

`"19:34"` returns `"19:39"`. `"23:59"` returns `"22:22"`.
SOLUTION:
```python
def next_closest_time(t: str) -> str:
    allowed = set(t.replace(":", ""))
    start = int(t[:2]) * 60 + int(t[3:])
    for step in range(1, 1441):
        minute = (start + step) % 1440
        candidate = f"{minute // 60:02d}{minute % 60:02d}"
        if set(candidate) <= allowed:
            return f"{candidate[:2]}:{candidate[2:]}"
```

There are only 1,440 minutes in a day, so trying each one going forward is constant time and impossible to get wrong. Step 1440 brings you back to the start, which handles the case where no other time works.

Google asked whether to brute-force the 1,440 minutes or generate permutations of the digits. Say that both are constant time here, that the minute walk is simpler and its correctness is obvious, and that you would only switch if the search space grew, for example with seconds or arbitrary digit sets. Choosing the simplest correct solution and saying why is a strong answer.

TITLE: Is the experiment result significant?
KIND: python
MIN: 20
TAG: modeling
PROBLEM:
Write `ab_test(conv_a, n_a, conv_b, n_b)` for a conversion experiment with control A and variant B. Return a tuple `(lift, z, p_value)`:

- `lift`: the relative change in conversion rate from A to B
- `z`: the two-proportion z statistic, using the pooled rate
- `p_value`: two-sided

Use only the standard library.
SOLUTION:
```python
from math import sqrt, erf

def ab_test(conv_a, n_a, conv_b, n_b):
    p_a, p_b = conv_a / n_a, conv_b / n_b
    pooled = (conv_a + conv_b) / (n_a + n_b)
    se = sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * (1 / n_a + 1 / n_b))
    z = (p_b - p_a) / se
    normal_cdf = 0.5 * (1 + erf(abs(z) / sqrt(2)))
    p_value = 2 * (1 - normal_cdf)
    lift = (p_b - p_a) / p_a
    return lift, z, p_value
```

Under the null hypothesis both arms share one conversion rate, estimated by pooling. The standard error of the difference follows from it, and z is the difference measured in standard errors. The normal distribution's cumulative function is expressed through `erf`, so no statistics library is needed.

With 200 of 1,000 against 240 of 1,000, the lift is 20%, z is about 2.16, and the two-sided p-value is about 0.03.

Be ready to say what the number does not tell you: whether the sample size was fixed in advance, whether the split was clean, whether a 20% lift on a small base matters commercially, and whether guardrail metrics held. A p-value is one input to a decision, not the decision.
